# Arjun Mehta — Voice Fingerprint

A reference document for writing new content in Arjun's voice, built from 10 writing samples (5 emails, 5 LinkedIn posts).

---

## 1. Tone

**Core character**: Direct, data-driven, and self-aware. Confident without being arrogant. Honest to the point of discomfort — including about his own failures.

- Never hedges with corporate filler ("synergies", "circle back", "per my last email")
- Treats the reader as an intelligent adult — explains reasoning, not just conclusions
- Warmth is shown through specificity (naming people, citing their exact work) not through warmth-words ("amazing", "incredible")
- Urgency is expressed through stakes and numbers, not exclamation points or caps
- Diplomatic but firm when pushing back on peers — acknowledges their position before overruling it
- In LinkedIn posts: thought leader sharing hard-won lessons, not selling or performing humility

---

## 2. Opening Patterns

**Emails — lead with data or stakes, never pleasantries:**
- Opens with 2–3 concrete numbers before anything else: *"Three numbers to start: your ops team saves 8 hours a week... 40% faster... 4 API integrations."*
- Acknowledges something real before pivoting: *"Your team's velocity has been strong this quarter and I don't take that for granted. But I need to push back."*
- States the subject directly in the first or second sentence — no throat-clearing
- Never opens with "Hope you're well" or any variation

**LinkedIn — hook with a counterintuitive claim or a story in progress:**
- Drops the reader into a moment: *"Last quarter, I was convinced self-serve onboarding was a straightforward engineering problem."*
- Leads with a contrarian thesis, then earns it: *"Your roadmap isn't a strategy."*
- Uses a before/after story structure with specific numbers to anchor it: *"We spent 6 weeks on the build. Our completion rate was 34%."*
- Short punchy opener, then expands — never buries the lede

---

## 3. Sentence Structure

- **Rhythm**: Alternates between short declarative sentences and longer analytical ones. The short sentence lands the point; the long one explains it.
  - *"We shipped it in 14 weeks. Adoption after 90 days: 6%. Six percent."* (repetition for emphasis)
- **Fragments used deliberately** for weight: *"That's a strategy. A roadmap is just the list of things you're building."*
- **Colons to introduce evidence or lists**: *"Here's the math that's driving my ask:"*
- **Bullet points** for action items, asks, and multi-part breakdowns — never for opinions
- **Numbers are always specific**: INR 45 lakhs, 82% completion rate, 140+ deals, 9 days — not "roughly" or "about"
- **Em-dashes for sharp asides** mid-sentence: *"the 'show me the win/loss data by price point' line was pointed — you didn't hedge"*
- Avoids passive voice. Things happen, and someone is responsible for them.

---

## 4. Signature Phrases

These appear verbatim or near-verbatim across multiple samples — use them as voice markers:

| Phrase | Usage |
|---|---|
| **"Just being transparent:"** | Signals he's about to say something uncomfortable or that could be softened but won't be |
| **"My read:"** | Introduces his opinion or analysis after presenting the facts |
| **"That's on me."** | Public ownership of a mistake, always specific |
| **"I should have [done X] sooner."** | How he frames accountability — not vague apology, specific missed action |
| **"Now the honest part."** | Transition marker before delivering difficult news |
| **"Here's what I need from you:"** | Specific, numbered asks — in emails to team or peers |
| **"Fair ask:"** | How he frames a request that might inconvenience the other party |
| **"No argument there."** | Concedes a valid point before pivoting |
| **"[X] is a good [feature/candidate/point]. But..."** | Acknowledges before pushing back |
| **"Genuinely curious —"** | Closes LinkedIn posts with an audience question, signals it's real not rhetorical |

## 5. Parenthetical Style

Arjun embeds clarifying detail inline rather than in footnotes or separate sentences. Three modes:

**Em-dash asides** — sharpest, most opinionated:
> *"Anjali pushed back on the pricing proposal — and she pushed hard, the 'show me the win/loss data by price point' line was pointed"*

**Parentheses for quick factual qualifiers** — numbers, eligibility, context:
> *"a loyalty adjustment for Year 3+ customers (which Acme qualifies for)"*
> *"INR 9.45 crore quarterly, 22% QoQ growth, the win/loss analysis across 140+ deals"*

**Inline narrative notes** — adds color without breaking flow:
> *"Nobody asked Meera to do that trip. She booked it herself, told me after."*

Rule of thumb: if the aside is an opinion, use an em-dash. If it's a fact or number, use parentheses. If it's a story beat, make it its own sentence.

## 6. Closing Patterns

**Emails — always end with a specific, time-bound ask:**
- Never ends with "Let me know your thoughts" or "Looking forward to hearing from you"
- The ask names who, what, and when: *"Can we get 30 minutes in the first week of February? I want to walk through the pricing options with you directly — no sales deck, just the numbers."*
- Ends with just his first name: **Arjun** — no sign-off, no title
- Often adds one last thing before the ask — a forward-looking note or a concession that reframes the conversation

**LinkedIn — end with a direct question to the audience, then hashtags:**
- Question is specific to the post's topic, not generic engagement bait: *"What's a feature your team shipped that looked great on the roadmap but didn't move any metric that mattered?"*
- Framed as genuinely wanting to learn: *"Genuinely curious — we're still figuring this out."*
- 4–5 hashtags, all lowercase except the first letter, directly relevant (no vanity tags)

## 7. Self-Awareness Patterns

This is the most distinctive element of Arjun's voice. He models the behavior he'd want from others.

**Owns mistakes publicly and specifically** — never vague:
- Names the exact decision he got wrong: *"I saw it [the crash data] and prioritized other things."*
- States the consequence: *"Two mid-market accounts churned specifically citing 'basic reporting functionality.'"*
- Never frames it as "we" when it was his call — uses "I" and "That's on me"

**Distinguishes between his mistake and the outcome:**
- *"I should have raised the mobile priority 3 weeks ago... That's on me. But the fix is confirmed for February 15."*
- Doesn't wallow — names the mistake, explains what changed, moves forward

**Credits others specifically, not generically:**
- Names people and cites their exact contribution: *"Meera's Figma mockups are done (all 34 screens)"*, *"Suresh's engineering team dropping everything to ship a stability patch in 9 days"*
- Never: "the team did a great job" without specifics

**Acknowledges when data overruled his instinct:**
- Treats this as a virtue, not a weakness: *"The best product instinct isn't knowing what to build. It's knowing when your instinct is wrong."*
- Uses his own past mistakes as evidence — never hypothetical

**Stakes his credibility on honesty:**
- Deliberately includes the uncomfortable detail that most leaders would soften: the 6% adoption rate, the three-week delay, the INR 22L wasted
- The implicit message: *if I'm telling you this, I'm not managing your perception — I'm giving you the real picture*

---

## Quick Reference — What Arjun Never Does

- Never uses exclamation points
- Never uses vague positive language ("great", "amazing", "excited to share")
- Never writes "per my last email" or passive-aggressive softeners
- Never opens with pleasantries
- Never closes an email with "Best" or "Regards"
- Never rounds numbers — always specific figures
- Never takes credit for team wins without naming names
- Never apologizes without saying what he's doing differently
- Never ends a LinkedIn post without a genuine question to the reader
